Strip away the branding and there are really only three ways to arrange power and data across a system: put it in one place, spread it across many independently-run places, or refuse to let it collect anywhere at all. Every platform, protocol, and political system I can think of is a version of one of these three, and I don't think we talk about them as clearly as we should.

## Option One: Centralised

This is the one we're all already living in. A handful of companies — the usual names, Google, Amazon, Facebook — end up holding a functional monopoly, not just over a market but over the data of every individual who passes through them. The individual gets none of the economic upside of that data and very little say in what happens to it. We're so used to this pattern that we don't just accept it for social platforms — we run our companies, our governments, our own institutions the same way, because centralising control is genuinely convenient. It's just not neutral. Convenience for the operator and leverage over everyone else tend to be the same thing wearing different names.

## Option Two: Federated

Mastodon is the example I keep coming back to. On paper it's decentralised — anyone can run their own instance, set their own rules, moderate their own community, bottom-up all the way down. In practice, usage skews hard toward a small number of large, well-known instances, and most of the network's activity ends up clustered around them anyway. So you get *some* of the benefit — no single company owns everyone's data, individual operators genuinely control their own slice — but the underlying gravity toward concentration doesn't disappear, it just relocates to a few popular nodes instead of one official one. It's decentralisation with an asterisk.

## Option Three: Peer-to-Peer

Then there's the option that refuses centralisation as a category — full peer-to-peer systems, encryption end to end, blockchains where every transaction verifies against the one before it rather than against a central authority's ledger. This is the version that actually protects anonymity and puts control back with the individual rather than any operator, popular or otherwise. It's also the version with the worst reputation, because the same properties that protect a whistleblower also protect someone doing something genuinely illegal, and that trade-off makes people uncomfortable enough to want to regulate the whole category out of existence.

I think that's the wrong instinct, and it's worth saying plainly: the fact that a tool *can* be misused by a small minority is not a reason to throw out the tool for everyone else using it legitimately. You don't dump the baby out with the bathwater because the bathwater is dirty. The actual work — and it is real work, not a slogan — is figuring out how you regulate decentralised systems, how you keep transactions secure and identities anonymous, without quietly re-centralising them in the process of trying to make them "safe."

## Why the Individual Is the Real Stakes

Underneath all three options is the same question: how much weight does a single individual actually carry inside a system built out of many individuals? That's true whether the system is a company, a financial network, or a society — every mass is made of people who each carry some real weight, and the way you architect a system either preserves that weight or quietly erodes it into the aggregate. Centralised systems erode it fastest, because a system with one point of control is also a system with exactly one point that decides how much any individual's data, vote, or voice actually counts. Decentralisation, even the messy, half-solved, federated-with-an-asterisk kind, is at least an attempt to keep that weight distributed instead of dissolved.

I don't have the security problem solved. I don't think anyone fully does yet. But I'm increasingly convinced the direction is right, even where the implementation isn't — and that's worth saying out loud before the argument gets reduced to "decentralisation is just for people with something to hide."
