Two unrelated thoughts occurred to me on the same evening, close enough together that they've stayed linked in my head ever since. Neither one is a big idea. Both are about the same quiet phenomenon: a communication tool gets designed for one purpose, and then the people using it at scale bend it into something the designer never quite intended.

## The "Haha" That Isn't Funny

Facebook's reactions were, on paper, about as straightforward as a feature gets. You can love a post, laugh at it, be amazed by it, be sad about it, be angry at it. A post that's meant to be funny gets the laughing face. Simple.

Except it didn't stay simple. People started using the laughing-face reaction on posts that weren't jokes at all — posts meant to mock someone, to humiliate them, to pile on. The reaction with the friendliest possible design intent, a face crying with laughter, became a way to publicly deride something without having to write a word. It's a strange kind of drift: the feature never changed, but the social meaning attached to it split in two, and now the same icon can mean "this delighted me" or "this is pathetic," depending entirely on who's using it and why. Nobody designed that second meaning. It just accumulated.

## SMS Was Never Supposed to Be a Conversation

The second thought is about text messaging generally, and it's less about any one platform. An SMS, in its original conception, was closer to a notice than a conversation — you send an alert, a heads-up, a single piece of information, not an invitation to sit down and chat. It wasn't built for back-and-forth in real time; it was built for "here's a thing you should know."

WhatsApp, Messenger, Instagram DMs — all of it inherited the *format* of that one-way notice but turned the *behavior* into something closer to a phone call stretched out over hours, except without any of a phone call's natural boundaries. A call either happens or it doesn't; you pick up or you don't, and either way it's over when you hang up. A chat conversation can open at a moment you genuinely don't want to talk, and then it just sits there, read receipt and all, generating a low hum of obligation to respond that a message designed as a one-way notice was never supposed to produce. We didn't ask for that pressure. It arrived as a side effect of turning a notice-board into a group chat.

## The Pattern Underneath Both

Neither of these is really about Facebook or about SMS specifically. They're both examples of the same thing: you can design a feature's mechanics carefully and still have no real control over the social behavior that grows on top of it once enough people are using it the same way at the same time. The tool doesn't get to keep its original intent just because that's what's written in the spec. It means whatever the crowd decides it means.
